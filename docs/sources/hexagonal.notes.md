# Hexagonal source notes

The National Park Service's [Columnar Jointing](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/volcanoes/columnar-jointing.htm), updated April 18, 2023, describes columnar joints as intersecting parallel fractures. Cooling causes contraction and fracturing; columns are commonly hexagonal, but can have three to seven sides. Its explanation describes three fractures intersecting at 120 degrees. This supports the basalt nature fact and the qualification that basalt columns are cooling-fracture structures, not literal disk packing.

Thomas C. Hales's primary paper [The Honeycomb Conjecture](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9906042), revised May 20, 2002, states and proves the equal-area planar partition minimum-perimeter result for regular hexagonal honeycomb. This is a mathematical optimization theorem, not a demonstration of how bees construct cells, and it is not the optimal disk-packing theorem.

The European Geosciences Union's [Features from the Field: Columnar Basalts and why Hexagons are Nature’s Favourite Shape](https://blogs.egu.eu/divisions/ts/2023/05/26/features-from-the-field-columnar-basalts-and-why-hexagons-are-natures-favourite-shape/), May 26, 2023, was also read. It supports cooling-induced tensile fractures and imperfect polygonal columns. The NPS explanation is preferred for the catalog citation because its geometric qualification is explicit and avoids treating every column as a regular hexagon.

The 60-degree catalog target describes successive central vertex directions of an ideal regular hexagon, not the 120-degree fracture-junction angle. A matching sampled polar angular gap cannot establish either biological or geological causation. These sources do not establish that the runtime implements a natural formation mechanism.
